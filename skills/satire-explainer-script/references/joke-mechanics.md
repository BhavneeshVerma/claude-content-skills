# Joke Mechanics (from 5 Jackpockets transcripts)

Each device has a name, how it works, real examples (short, for study only, never reuse) and a build recipe. Target density: one laugh beat every 10 to 15 seconds, and never two of the same device back to back.

## 1. The mock-honorific
Give a criminal or absurd person a formal, respectful title. The gap between title and truth is the joke.
- "world famous pharmaceutical salesman El Chapo"
- "creative visionary Kanye West", "rich person number 15" (Elon), "the sexiest hedge fund of all time"
- **Recipe:** [corporate or LinkedIn job title that is technically true] + [name]. Deliver it flat.

## 2. The stinger list (rule of three, last item breaks)
Two normal items, then a third that is dark, petty or anticlimactic.
- Queen perks: jewelry collection, Bentley without a license, "pay taxes when you feel like it"
- Trump's changes: an 80ft flag in the yard, then "in his bathroom he added a few classified documents"
- Casio F91W "worn by Osama Bin Laden and Osama Bin Laden's bombs"
- "your dream watch, your dream fit, your dream grocery haul"
- **Recipe:** keep the rhythm identical across all three items so the ear expects a match, then break it on item three.

## 3. The flat understatement
Say a horrifying fact, then react as if it were mildly inconvenient. Or invert severity.
- "...kicked out for hitting on a teenager. Classic Jeffrey."
- After an assassination: "that might be an awkward conversation. It's pretty cool house though."
- "you might end up getting killed, or even worse, divorced"
- **Recipe:** the dark fact first, then a reaction scaled for a minor social faux pas.

## 4. "In unrelated news" (implication by juxtaposition)
Never state the accusation. Line up the facts and let the viewer finish the thought.
- Cyanide death, "no suspect was ever found. In unrelated news, these two women inherited all of his winnings. In completely unrelated news, this woman prepared the last meal he ate."
- Jack Ma "made the grave mistake of criticizing the Chinese government" [pause] "Jack Ma isn't dead by the way"
- **Recipe:** fact, fact, fact, with escalating "unrelated" tags. The audience gets the satisfaction of solving it. This is also the legally safest way to be edgy.

## 5. The idiom gone literal
Take a car, money or status idiom and land it on the literal subject.
- "You'll have to ride Ferrari if you want to ride in a Ferrari"
- "one small step for man, one giant leap for the Omega marketing team"
- Mitsubishi (stunt cars) "decided to jump off a cliff" (its business)
- Camouflage wrap "hides him from traffic laws", chocolate-dipped car "looked so sweet he started doing ad libs"
- **Recipe:** list 5 idioms connected to the subject, pick the one whose literal reading is also true. These are language-bound, so Hindi needs its own muhavare (see hindi-and-legal.md).

## 6. The sincere setup, real pull
Build a wholesome or moral message, then let a fact or clip demolish it.
- Buffett says his house was a great investment "because of the memories it brought him and his family" [6s of silence, a visual gag]
- "This clip made me love Jeff... you don't need a fancy house or a nice car. Jeffrey Preston Bezos [beat] once drove a 1997 Honda Accord. 20 years later his car collection is valued at over 20 million."
- **Recipe:** play the sentiment completely straight for 2 to 3 lines, with no wink. The longer it runs, the bigger the drop.

## 7. The long-game plant (Chekhov's character)
Introduce a helper as a hero early, keep him likeable, and reveal the betrayal late.
- Lottery: "I found you this guy, Jason Kurland," then "our boy Jason," trusted with your money for 8 minutes, then news clip: lottery lawyer charged with extorting winners. "In hindsight, giving this guy access to our finances was a pretty stupid decision."
- **Recipe:** plant in the first 20%, reference warmly 2 to 3 times, pay off in the last 25%. This is the strongest retention device in the set.

## 8. The runner (repeated line that evolves)
A phrase that comes back 3 times, changing meaning each time.
- Watches: "This is all slightly absurd, you don't need a watch nowadays" (dismissal), then the same line on a Rolex (doubt), then "maybe this is all slightly absurd, but maybe that's the point" (thesis)
- "Inspiration comes at odd times... we can make a watch out of this" (Cartier Tank from war tanks, then Cartier Crash from a car crash, then debunked: "rich people got bored")
- Drake clip "pretentious personally, but hey if you have the money, feel free" turned into the closing verdict on Drake himself
- **Recipe:** pick a line in the first third, repeat it mid-video in a new context, and land it last with an inverted meaning.

## 9. The clip as punchline
The narrator sets up a line and a real clip (interview, news, vlog) delivers it. The narrator never says the joke.
- "creative visionary Kanye said this:" then the clip "everybody house whack, it's trash"
- Accountant warns Ellison to budget, then a news clip: "he's just bought the island of Lanai"
- Kanye's SLR, then a clip saying "shut the f up". "My daily driver is a Civic Type R," then "Shut up."
- MrBeast's i8 in front of a trap house, then his own old video: "Today we are going to rob this house"
- **Recipe:** write the setup so it needs an answer, then find the clip that answers it. This is archive work and needs a footage researcher (see the blind spots in SKILL.md).

## 10. The shock tag (handle with care)
A neutral fact with a dark tag bolted on.
- "fully blacked out, just like Diddy's victims", "thrown out the window faster than an Apple sweatshop worker", "they already have tents for that", "closer to his family and further from the IRS"
- **Rule:** punch up (billionaires, corporations, the powerful) only. Several of his tags punch down (ethnicity, poverty, African child labor). Do not adapt those: they are the part with the most legal and brand risk and the least comedic value.

## 11. Arbitrary tier logic (the verdict is the joke)
The ranking system is visibly broken, and that is the running gag.
- "B tier, but he's also evading taxes so that bumps him up to A tier"
- "a D tier car with S tier lore, so it's going in B tier"
- "Kevin didn't make any money off his car crash, so he only gets A tier"
- "for eight million dollars I would rather just buy the state of Wisconsin"
- God tier tease: "God tier only has room for one person... it is not Gordon Ramsay" (an open loop, paid off with the Pope)
- **Recipe:** every verdict carries a reason, and the reason is either petty, moral-inverted or a callback.

## 12. Mock precision and pop references
Fake exact numbers and nerd references in a deadpan voice.
- "57 quintillion Omega Speedmaster models", "took him 16.8 seconds to exit", "polishing instructions written by the Half Blood Prince"
- **Recipe:** one absurd number per segment, stated like a fact.

## 13. The broke-narrator persona
The narrator is one of us, judging them.
- "to the average broke person like myself", "I think I'm too poor to even be looking at this house", "my boy Kenneth", "our boy Jason"
- The persona licenses the mockery: it reads as envy-flavored honesty, not snobbery.

## 14. The quiet thesis under the jokes
Every video ends on a real idea about wealth and status, said almost sincerely.
- Watches: "a small trinket that tells the time can be a toy, a tool, a status symbol and even a work of art," and the $50M watch runs on the same quartz movement as your $30 kids' watch
- Lottery: the curse is a myth, "maybe it just makes us feel better if we think they are"
- House: Elon "got rid of his stuff," but that was 0.05% of his wealth, "he still has a lot of stuff"
- This is why the channel feels smart, not just snarky. Do not skip it.

## 15. Scale translation (the core finance device)
Turn a number too big to feel into something you can feel.
- "you spending 240 million is like an average person spending $60"
- a stack of $1 bills to the top of the Burj Khalifa, then "you still need 87,000 times that"
- "your passive income earns you $400 every 0.126 seconds"; toilet paper made of $39,000 bills
- a banana-antimatter calculation that takes "97 times the age of the universe"
- **Recipe:** big number, then a ratio to a viewer's daily unit (salary, chai, EMI, rent, an hour of life). Every finance or health statistic gets one.

## 16. The game with a rule (the premise as a constraint)
Give the video a rule and a timer, then let reality break the plan.
- "spend all of it in the next 24 hours or you lose everything", then "Transaction failed", then "the Metropolitan Museum is... not owned by... You know what? Forget about the Met."
- pay.gov accepts at most $1 million: "You just bought the government a bag of screws from Lockheed Martin."
- **Recipe:** state the rule in the hook. Every 60 to 90 seconds, a real-world fact blocks the plan. That fact is the lesson.

## 17. Dramatized history (cheap skit)
Retell a historical deal as a two-line modern conversation in silly voices.
- Da Vinci: "Could you make me a painting of my wife?" "Sure. Give me like... 2 weeks." [gap: the painting is never finished]
- Napoleon: "HOW ABOUT YOU PAY ME 15 MILLION FOR THE WHOLE THING?" "Y- Yes."
- **Recipe:** turn any boring "in 1803..." passage into 2 lines of dialogue.

## 18. The sincere awe pivot
Drop the jokes for 20 to 30 seconds to say something sincerely beautiful, then pivot with an antonym.
- ISS: "what can be accomplished when the world comes together. But it's way more costly when the world comes apart. War."
- Ending: the Sun "worth 30 nonillion... an infinite amount of wealth, all of which we got for free."
- This is what makes viewers stay to the end and share. In health content it is the "your parents' knees" moment.

## 19. Ad-voice parody (then the drop)
Describe a cheap or ordinary thing in sincere, glowing ad copy, with no wink, then drop one real, dark or deflating fact on it.
- Flik Flak: "unbeatable quality with great durability and elegant designs. It's the perfect start to your watch owning journey." (a $30 kids' watch)
- Casio: "a brand that sets the standard... Timeless designs. The legendary F91W was worn throughout history by highly influential figures like Osama Bin Laden. And Osama Bin Laden's bombs."
- **Why it works:** the viewer hears an ad, relaxes, then the fact lands. The ad tone only works as parody. Without the drop it IS an ad. Never write ad copy without a drop within 2 lines.

## Format skeletons (mirror these beat by beat before writing anything)

### "Every Level / N Levels Of X" (source: Every Level Of Watches)
1. **Two extreme top-end items, no explanation.** "This is a $1.5 million watch. This one costs 50 million."
2. **"But your journey begins here, with [cheapest item]."** Second person, starting in childhood.
3. **Ad-voice parody of the cheap item** (device 19), then its dark or real fact.
4. **Life-stage trigger to the next level:** "But as you get older you want something cooler, so for [price] you buy [item]." Status anxiety, not need, moves you up.
5. Each level: one real history or industry fact, one deadpan tag, one reason to leave it.
6. Runner from the middle on ("This is all slightly absurd...").
7. **Bookend:** the top level secretly contains the bottom level (the $50M watch runs on the same quartz movement as the Flik Flak).

**Never in this format:** an agenda line ("aaj hum dekhenge..."), a social lecture, "sabka ek hi sapna", or a list of prices with no story. The hook IS the start of the journey.

### "Tier List / N Tiers Of X" (source: House, Yacht, Celebrity Car tier lists)
1. **Range line in the first 4 seconds:** "From [cheapest] to [priciest], [optional weird third], this is the X tier list." The third item is the stinger.
2. **Straight into entry one.** No intro, no agenda. One fact, one twist, one verdict, and on to the next.
3. Each tier opens on a real, specific moment the viewer lived, then the fact under it.

## Relatability rule (applies to every format)
Exaggerate a thing the audience actually did, never invent a thing they did not do. Test: would most of the target viewers say "haan, maine bhi kiya"? Real small details (face wash in the school bag, recess washroom, wiping your face with the same dirty hanky) beat big invented ones (the whole family shares one soap). Gross-out hygiene jokes read as slapstick: cut them.

## Structural patterns

**The escalation ladder (the format of the biggest video):** one question in the title, answered only at the end. Each step is "But X is even more [expensive / dangerous / effective]." Mix categories (art, then rock, then land, then house, then megaproject, then war, then antimatter) so every step is a surprise. After the answer, one more twist zooms out (country, Earth, universe). "But" opens a sentence about 2.4 times per minute. It is the engine of retention.

**Hook (first 10 seconds), one of three shapes:**
- Range: "From a $2 billion mega mansion to a $50,000 tiny home, this is the billionaire house tier list."
- Paradox question: "Zuckerberg could buy a new Lamborghini every day for life... so why does he drive a Honda Fit?"
- Second-person shock: "You just won the lottery. Congrats. If you're lucky enough to survive the lawsuits, betrayals and murder attempts, you might just live long enough to go bankrupt."

**Bookend:** the hook's extreme comes back as the ending. The $50K tiny home opens and closes House. The $30 Flik Flak opens Watches and returns as the quartz reveal. The "lottery curse" opens Lottery and closes as the myth-bust.

**Pacing:** long story entries (40 to 90s, with 3 to 5 beats and a twist) alternate with rapid-fire one-liners (3 to 6s: "Michael Bloomberg drives a Chevy Suburban." "Jamie Foxx drives a tank. Seems pretty inconvenient. C tier.") so the rhythm never settles.

**Entry anatomy (list formats):** credential or net worth, then the surprising fact, 2 to 3 escalating specifics with real numbers, then the twist or clip, then a verdict with a joke reason. About 70% of each entry is real, checkable information. The humor rides on credibility.

**Second-person journey:** "you" is the protagonist. Each step raises the price or the stakes. Each level gets a reason to leave it ("but as you get older you want something cooler"). Twist in the last quarter, then a reframing ending.
